It’s your phone, not theirs

Be free to install the apps you choose, from the people you choose, without asking Google first.

Why our apps aren’t on Google Play

Every app we make is free, open source and private: no ads, no tracking, no account, and nothing leaves your device. Google Play would add nothing to that, and it would cost something: to publish there, a developer has to register with Google and hand over government ID.

Now Google wants that same registration for apps that never go near the Play Store. We think the person who owns the phone should decide what runs on it, so we publish our apps on our own website and on GitHub, where anyone can read every line of the code.

What Google is changing

NOW
30 Sep 2026Store apps checked in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand
2027 (planned)Every app checked, on certified Android phones everywhere
  • Since 30 September 2026, in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, Android phones with Google’s services check that apps from the big app stores (Google Play, Samsung, Xiaomi and others) come from a developer registered with Google. Apps downloaded straight from a developer’s website still install as before: we tested all of ours on a stock Pixel 6 Pro in Thailand in November 2026.
  • In 2027, Google plans to check every app on certified Android phones everywhere, wherever it comes from.
  • After that, apps from developers who haven’t registered can still be installed, but by a slower route: an “advanced” setting in Developer options with a one-time 24-hour wait, or over a USB cable from a computer (ADB), which isn’t affected.

Google says this protects people from scams. Scams are real, but the answer shouldn’t be one company deciding which developers people are allowed to trust.

Imagine your front door. A good lock keeps out the people you don’t want. A lock where only the landlord holds the key, and decides who you can let in isn’t protecting you — it’s controlling you. Security should be a lock you hold the key to, not one someone else holds for you.

Holding your own key means deciding who to let in. That’s a skill, not a risk, and the checks below take about two minutes to learn. The Keep Android Open campaign explains the case well.

Step by step

  1. Get it from the developer’s own page. For our apps, that’s the Android page linked from each app (for example Redact’s), or our GitHub releases.
  2. Allow the install. The first time, Android asks you to let your browser or file manager install apps. Allow it for that app only.
  3. Check it’s the real thing. Each of our Android pages shows the app’s signing certificate fingerprint. Once an app is installed, Android will only accept updates signed with the same certificate, so a fake can’t replace it. (A signing certificate is like a wax seal on a letter: the developer seals every app with a key only they hold, and Android refuses any update with a different seal.)
  4. Keep it up to date. Obtainium watches our GitHub releases and installs updates for you. Our apps can also check for updates themselves (About → Check for updates).

Or skip all of that with a computer: our Installer installs our apps over a USB cable, straight from your browser. It checks each app’s checksum and certificate before installing, and because it uses USB, Google’s new checks won’t slow it down, now or in 2027.

Is this app worth trusting?

Nobody checks your groceries for you, and nobody needs to: you’ve learned to read a label. Apps are the same. A few habits are all it takes to choose well.

  • Is it open source? Anyone can read the code, so it’s much harder to hide something bad in it.
  • Which permissions does it ask for? A photo editor doesn’t need your contacts. None of our apps has internet access.
  • Does the developer publish a signing certificate? Then you can check that the app you have is theirs.
  • Where does it come from? The developer’s own site, GitHub releases, or a store like F-Droid that builds apps from their source code. Be wary of “APK mirror” sites that repackage apps.
